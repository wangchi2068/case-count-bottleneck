\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The return on expanding case volume in epidemic surveillance depends fundamentally on the inferential target. For the conditional instantaneous reproduction number $R_t$, returns to scale do not saturate: estimation variance decays strictly as $1/m$, attaining the Cram\'er--Rao lower bound (Proposition~\ref{prop:current}). For the cross-period mean $\Rbar$, however, estimation error contains two components that do not attenuate with sample volume: environmental variance $v_R$, arising from shared meteorological, social, and mobility fluctuations across periods; and reporting heterogeneity variance $v_H$, arising from temporal instability in ascertainment (Propositions~\ref{prop:average} and~\ref{prop:report-noise}). The variance balance scale $m_\times = A/v_R$ (compressed to $A_H/(v_R+v_H)$ under reporting noise) defines the scale where sampling noise balances the environmental floor. Parag and colleagues \citep{parag2022quantifying, parag2022fundamental} characterized the sampling-dependent component from an information-theoretic perspective; our results show that environmental stochasticity introduces an extrinsic floor that operates independently of population volume, providing a surveillance-design analogue to classical ecological distinctions between demographic and environmental stochasticity \citep{engen1998demographic, rohani2002determinism}.

Our inferential targets must be distinguished from the apparent reproduction numbers analysed by \citet{eales2024differences}. \citet{eales2024differences} demonstrated that downstream surveillance time series (such as hospitalisations, intensive care admissions, or deaths) reconstruct an apparent reproduction number that is delayed and smoothed relative to the true transmission rate due to convolution with clinical delay distributions. In our framework, Target 1 is the conditional instantaneous transmission rate and Target 2 is the cross-period mean. Downstream surveillance outcomes need not reproduce the temporal dynamics of infection incidence without distortion \citep{eales2024differences}. We therefore interpret our hospitalisation analysis as an empirical assessment of error scaling in a surveillance-derived growth statistic, rather than as a direct validation of the theoretical error decomposition for the latent reproduction number. While deconvolution techniques can mitigate apparent delay and smoothing, they cannot remove environmental variance from Target 2, because environmental fluctuations represent physical variation in transmission rather than an artefact of lagged observation.

The theoretical lower bounds established here apply strictly to single-period, conditionally unbiased estimators of $R_t$ and to one-step forecasts under independent environments. Estimators that tolerate conditional bias---such as James--Stein shrinkage, empirical Bayes methods \citep{efron1975data}, and state-space smoothers like EpiFilter \citep{parag2021improved}---can achieve a mean squared error below $v_R$ by shrinking estimates toward a global mean $\Rbar$. For example, under baseline parameters with $m = 50$, linear shrinkage achieves MSE below $v_R = 0.04$ whenever the prior mean is within 0.279 of $\Rbar$, and achieves MSE of $0.0089$ (22\% of $v_R$) when the prior mean is within 0.1 of $\Rbar$ (Supplementary Section~S1.11). However, this variance reduction comes at the cost of conditional bias that peaks precisely when $R_t$ deviates furthest from normal transmission, which is when outbreak detection is most urgent. Sizing surveillance under biased estimators requires explicit loss trade-offs that lie outside the conditionally unbiased framework.

For surveillance design, our three levers target distinct error components. Expanding single-period sample volume suppresses sampling variance, but its value diminishes rapidly once volume reaches the scale of $m_\times$. Accumulating observation periods or pooling across jurisdictions suppresses the environmental term; indeed, the estimation precision of $v_R$ itself improves solely with period count $T$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:vR-info}). Standardising reporting protocols or recording institutional reporting completeness eliminates reporting heterogeneity $v_H$. In baseline calculations, quadrupling period count reduces total MSE by 72\% (from $22.5\times 10^{-3}$ to $6.2\times 10^{-3}$), whereas increasing single-period sample volume tenfold reduces MSE by 17\% (to $18.7\times 10^{-3}$). When environmental shocks exhibit positive autocorrelation, surveillance design must account for the budget threshold $\mathcal{B}^\dagger$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:ar-design}): small-budget systems should prioritize fewer, larger observation windows to avoid severe under-sampling per generation.

Surveillance practice should decouple early warning from trend estimation. Outbreak detection ($R_t > 1$) is an instance of Target 1, where sampling volume maintains continuous value. At small scales, exact tests should replace Wald tests to prevent size distortion, and surveillance capacity must be sized according to detection power curves (Section~\ref{sec:res-test}) and deployed to sentinel facilities with rapid, complete reporting. Conversely, tracking baseline transmission trends represents Target 2; once capacity approaches $m_\times$, additional resources are far more effectively allocated to temporal replication, broader geographic pooling, or reporting standardisation.

Several methodological limitations warrant emphasis. First, weekly hospital admissions aggregate approximately two influenza generations, meaning empirical regression coefficients $a$ and $b$ capture composite scaling shapes rather than structural single-generation biological parameters. Second, while model comparisons decisively favor models with non-vanishing error over floorless specifications, current sample sizes cannot fully separate a strictly flat asymptotic plateau from slow power-law decay ($\hat{p} = 0.273$). Third, empirical intercepts reflect unmodelled error components not explained by sample volume and cannot be uniquely attributed to environmental variance; our hospital reporting completeness proxy accounts for $<1\%$ of intercept excess, but cannot capture within-hospital underreporting or behavioral shifts in care-seeking. Fourth, seasonal transmission variance is fundamentally constrained by the number of observed epidemic seasons: by Corollary~\ref{cor:season}, three seasons bound the relative standard error of seasonal variance from below by 0.82, precluding precise estimation of multi-year baseline stability without extended longitudinal archives.
