\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

When the target of surveillance is typical transmission or next period's value, the precision of a case-count estimate of the reproduction number grows with case volume at a diminishing rate. Beyond the balance scale, variation shared within a period dominates and each further doubling of cases removes at most a quarter of the remaining variance. In US influenza hospital admissions, an affine model placed the apparent balance ratio at about 37 admissions per state-week on the scale of a renewal-type ratio, although a power law without a floor fitted about as well. After calibration by simulation, the implied environmental variance was about 0.04 under the reference scenario. This value assumes the 3.6-day serial interval as generation interval and weekly shocks without autocorrelation; shorter generation intervals, which direct estimates favour, would make it smaller and the latent balance scale larger. A large part of the shared variation was common to all states in a week. Where the affine description holds, surveillance systems operating with case volumes above the price-adjusted threshold $m^*$ gain more precision from additional observation periods and weakly synchronised regions than from expanding cases per period. The evidence on reporting is narrower: within the high-completeness mandatory reporting windows, facility participation changed the intercept little, and the comparison with the voluntary period of 2024 was inconclusive. Empirical intercepts of apparent statistics should be reported together with the calibration that links them to transmission and with the functional form on which they depend.
