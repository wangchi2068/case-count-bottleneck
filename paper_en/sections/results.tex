\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The results are arranged in three layers. Sections~\ref{sec:res-theory} and \ref{sec:res-allocation} check the theory in simulations where the truth is known. Sections~\ref{sec:res-empirical} to \ref{sec:res-fe} describe how the loss of a weekly statistic varies with case volume in NHSN admissions and test that relation by thinning the counts and by fitting state intercepts. Sections~\ref{sec:res-sim} to \ref{sec:res-design} calibrate the empirical intercepts against the latent model and trace the consequences for reporting and design.

\subsection{Two targets with known truth}
\label{sec:res-theory}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_01_concept_error.pdf}
  \caption{Two inferential targets for a case-count estimate of the reproduction number. (A) Schematic. For the current value $R_t$, the interval of each period's estimate narrows as more cases are counted. For the mean across periods, $\Rbar$, the deviations $R_t-\Rbar$ (arrows), whose variance is $v_R$, remain however many cases are counted. (B) Mean squared error of $\protect\Rhat_t=C_t/m$ about $R_t$ and about $\Rbar$ against expected reported cases per period, at $\Rbar=1.15$, $v_R=0.04$, $k=0.35$ and $\rho=0.25$. Lines are the closed forms $A/m$ and $v_R+A/m$; circles are Monte Carlo estimates from 200000 draws per value, with 95\% Monte Carlo intervals mostly hidden by the symbols. The dotted line marks $v_R$ and the dashed line the balance scale $\mx=53.1$.}
  \label{fig:concept}
\end{figure}

Under the baseline parameters ($\Rbar=1.15$, $v_R=0.04$, $k=0.35$, $\rho=0.25$), the sampling coefficient was $A=2.1232$ and the balance scale was $\mx=53.1$ reported cases per period (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}B). The mean squared error about the current value fell in proportion to $1/m$ over the whole range, whereas the error about the mean levelled off at $v_R$. Across 22 case volumes from 2 to 5000, Monte Carlo estimates of both errors differed from $A/m$ and $v_R+A/m$ by at most 0.74\%, so the two curves were separated by $v_R$ throughout. Doubling the case volume reduced the error about the mean by 42.1\% at $m=10$, by 25.0\% at $m=\mx$ and by 10.5\% at $m=200$.

The formulas of Corollary~\ref{cor:targets} for the process mean over ten periods, the finite-period mean and the naive and best linear one-step forecasts matched simulation to within 1.39\% at $\phi=0$, 0.5 and 0.8. Applying the formula for independent periods, $v_R/T+A/(Tm)$, to autocorrelated periods departed from the simulated error by up to 430\%, which is the size of the correction $S(\phi,T)$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:ar-sum}).

Raising reporting probability at fixed coverage did not reduce error about the mean to $v_R$. At full reporting the residual variance was 0.166 with 40 primary cases, 0.0652 with 200 and 0.0450 with 1000, against $v_R=0.04$. Near the baseline, a proportional increase in coverage reduced error 1.85 times as much as the same proportional increase in reporting probability.

\subsection{Reporting heterogeneity and the allocation of a budget}
\label{sec:res-allocation}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_02_reporting_allocation.pdf}
  \caption{Reporting heterogeneity and the allocation of a fixed budget. (A) Error variance about the mean across periods, $V_H(m)$, for relative reporting volatility $\tau$ of 0, 0.1, 0.15 and 0.2. Dotted lines mark the limits $v_R+v_H$ and open circles the generalised balance scale $m_{\times,H}$. (B) Optimal reported cases per period against the ratio of cost per period to cost per case, $c_0/c_1$. Solid lines give $m^*=\sqrt{(c_0/c_1)m_{\times,H}}$ for independent $R_t$ at $\tau=0$ and 0.15. Dashed lines give the numerical optimum under a first-order autoregressive $R_t$ with $\phi=0.8$ for budgets of 1000 and 10000 in units of the cost per case; designs with a single period are omitted.}
  \label{fig:allocation}
\end{figure}

A relative reporting volatility of $\tau=0.15$ added $v_H=0.0307$ to the limiting variance and reduced the balance scale from 53.1 to 30.4 cases (Fig.~\ref{fig:allocation}A). Eq.~(\ref{eq:mx-gap}) reproduced this difference to rounding error over the range of $\tau$ examined, and simulated $V_H(m)$ with beta-distributed reporting differed from Eq.~(\ref{eq:vH-AH}) by at most 0.23\%.

With independent periods, linear costs and continuous $T$, a grid search over designs located the optimum within 0.03\% of Eq.~(\ref{eq:optimal-m}), and at every budget examined the error increased with $m$ exactly when $m>m^*$. Under first-order autocorrelation the rule no longer held. For $\phi=0.5$ and 0.8, cost ratios of 10 and 100 and budgets from 300 to 30000, the numerical optimum ranged from 0.33 to 2.63 times $m^*$, and it depended on the budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:allocation}B). The scenario parameters for influenza admissions give uncorrected balance scales of 21.3 to 49.2 cases per generation (Section~\ref{sec:meth-loss}); over this range, $m^*$ was 14.6 to 22.2 cases per period for $c_0/c_1=10$ and 46.2 to 70.1 cases for $c_0/c_1=100$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget}C).

In a hypothetical example with ten periods of 50 cases, autocorrelated environment and reporting ($\phi=\psi=0.5$) and $\tau=0.15$, the mean squared error of the mean was $22.57\times10^{-3}$, of which $10.40\times10^{-3}$ was environmental, $7.88\times10^{-3}$ reporting and $4.29\times10^{-3}$ sampling (Table~\ref{tab:s7}). A tenfold increase in case volume reduced it to $18.71\times10^{-3}$, whereas standardising reporting reduced it to $14.65\times10^{-3}$ and four times as many periods to $6.17\times10^{-3}$. At twice the baseline budget, spending the increment on cases gave $19.35\times10^{-3}$ and spending it on periods gave $11.98\times10^{-3}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget}D).

\subsection{Loss and case volume in NHSN admissions}
\label{sec:res-empirical}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_03_nhsn_identification.pdf}
  \caption{Relation between loss and case volume in NHSN influenza admissions and two tests of it. (A) Relative loss of the renewal-type ratio $R_A$ against weekly admissions $C_t$ for 1748 state-weeks. Black points are means of 12 quantile bins with $\pm1.96$ standard errors that ignore clustering. The solid line is the affine fit $a+b/C_t$ ($b/a=37.4$) and the dashed line the power law $cC_t{}^{-\gamma}$ ($\gamma=0.38$). (B) Binomial thinning of the observed counts. The upper panel gives the slope on the original scale, $b(q)$, against the inverse retention probability $1/q$, with the Poisson prediction $b(1)+0.446(1/q-1)$ as a dashed line. The lower panel gives the intercept $a(q)$, with $a(1)$ as a dashed line. Bands are 95\% two-way bootstrap intervals. (C) Apparent balance ratio of the pooled cross-sectional fit and of fits with state intercepts (FE), in which the within-state slope is divided by the mean, median, row-weighted or case-weighted state intercept, with 95\% two-way bootstrap intervals from 1000 draws.}
  \label{fig:identification}
\end{figure}

In the rule-defined early-growth windows of four seasons, 1748 state-weeks in 42 calendar weeks gave a renewal-type ratio intercept of $a=0.0150$ (95\% CI 0.0100 to 0.0208), slope $b=0.560$ (95\% CI 0.367 to 0.769) and $b/a=37.4$ (95\% CI 19.1 to 71.0) (Fig.~\ref{fig:identification}A; Table~\ref{tab:s1}). The weighted growth ratio in the same windows gave $a=0.0840$ (95\% CI 0.0498 to 0.1248) and $b/a=23.5$ (95\% CI 8.4 to 59.7) from 1756 state-weeks. The eight additional state-weeks have a reporting scale $D_t$ of at least five but fewer than five admissions in the current week, which $R_A$ requires because its scale is the single-week count. In the three calendar windows of the earlier analysis, the growth ratio gave $a=0.0991$ (95\% CI 0.0569 to 0.1382) and $b/a=22.6$ (95\% CI 9.0 to 52.2). Within the affine model, all bootstrap replicates gave a positive intercept for both rule-window specifications, as did 99.95\% for the calendar windows. The bootstrap standard deviation of $a$ was 1.95 to 4.68 times that obtained by resampling states only.

The ratio $b/a$ varied across specifications by a factor of 3.9 for $R_A$, from 24.7 after trimming the most influential 1\% of losses to 97.1 in peak weeks, and by a factor of 7.1 for $G$, from 12.2 with equal denominator weights to 86.8 in peak weeks (Fig.~\ref{fig:floor}D). Generation interval variants changed it by less than 3\% for $R_A$. Over the observed range of scales, a power law without an intercept, with exponent $\gamma=0.38$ for $R_A$, fitted the binned losses about as well as the affine form (Fig.~\ref{fig:identification}A; bootstrap $p=0.114$ for $R_A$, 0.438 and 0.418 for $G$; Table~\ref{tab:s2}). Cross-validation by state and by season did not consistently favour the affine form, and a power analysis found no fraction of the estimated intercept detectable with 80\% power (Table~\ref{tab:s2}). The data therefore do not distinguish a positive floor from a loss that keeps declining as a power of the reporting scale. The affine form was retained as a working point because its parameters correspond to Proposition~\ref{prop:average} and enter the calibration directly. Under the power law no balance scale exists, so the intercept, the apparent balance ratio and the quantities derived from them below are conditional on the affine form.

\subsection{Binomial thinning}
\label{sec:res-thinning}

Thinning the weekly counts raised the slope on the original scale from 0.560 without thinning to 4.01 at $q=0.1$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:identification}B; Table~\ref{tab:s11}). The pooled rate was $c=0.407$ (95\% CI 0.311 to 0.531), compatible with the Poisson value $b_P=0.446$; the ratio $c/b_P$ was 0.91 (95\% CI 0.69 to 1.19). With a free intercept the rate was 0.386 (95\% CI 0.267 to 0.530). The rate at each level was 0.47 to 0.50 for $q\ge0.2$ and 0.383 at $q=0.1$, where many thinned counts are small. In the 828 state-weeks with $C_t\ge50$ it was 0.477 (95\% CI 0.286 to 0.769), against a Poisson value of 0.420 in that subset.

No change in the intercept was detected. At every level the 95\% interval of $a(q)-a(1)$ included zero, for example $-1.3\times10^{-3}$ (95\% CI $-4.8\times10^{-3}$ to $1.8\times10^{-3}$) at $q=0.5$ and $0.4\times10^{-3}$ (95\% CI $-7.0\times10^{-3}$ to $11.9\times10^{-3}$) at $q=0.1$. These intervals are wide relative to $a(1)=0.0150$, so the analysis excludes only large changes. Fitted as $\alpha+\beta/q$, the mean loss over the sample had $\alpha=0.0301$ (95\% CI 0.0189 to 0.0417) and $\beta=0.0117$ (95\% CI 0.0086 to 0.0154), so 72\% (95\% CI 58 to 81) of the unthinned mean loss did not scale with $1/q$.

Because $b(1)=0.560$ exceeds $c$, the slope on the thinned scale, $qb(q)$, fell from 0.56 to 0.40 as $q$ decreased. Under Proposition~\ref{prop:average} thinning amplifies only the reporting part of $A/m$, whereas the offspring part, $(\Rbar^2+v_R)/(kn)$, does not change with $\rho$; the excess of $b(1)$ over $c$ is therefore consistent with a contribution of offspring overdispersion to the slope. Thinning tests the scaling of the term that falls with case volume. It leaves transmission and reporting volatility unchanged and therefore cannot separate them within the intercept.

\subsection{Fits with state intercepts}
\label{sec:res-fe}

With 51 state intercepts the within-state slope was $b_{\mathrm{w}}=0.379$ (95\% CI 0.104 to 0.669) and the mean state intercept $\bar{a}_s=0.0219$ (95\% CI 0.0124 to 0.0351), giving a within-state ratio of 17.3 (95\% CI 3.3 to 51.4), against 37.4 for the pooled cross-section (Fig.~\ref{fig:identification}C; Table~\ref{tab:s11}). The ratio was 21.7 with the median state intercept, 18.4 with the row-weighted mean and 24.6 with the case-weighted mean, and 17.7 (95\% CI 3.2 to 51.4) with $D_t$ as the scale. No state intercept was negative. Within the same bootstrap draws, the ratio of the cross-sectional to the within-state value was 2.16 (95\% CI 0.93 to 7.75), so the difference is not distinguished from sampling variation.

State intercepts correlated negatively with the logarithm of the state median count, $r=-0.44$ (95\% CI $-0.67$ to 0.08). Larger states thus tended to have lower intercepts, as spatial averaging of environmental variation over more local epidemics would produce (Eq.~(\ref{eq:pooling})), but the interval includes zero. With week intercepts in place of state intercepts the slope was 0.508 (95\% CI 0.232 to 0.835). With both sets of intercepts the slope was 0.78 (95\% CI $-1.20$ to 2.77) and was not identified, because removing state and week means left 41\% of the variance of $1/C_t$, against 74\% after removing state means alone.

\subsection{Calibration and the implied environmental variance}
\label{sec:res-sim}

The empirical statistics are apparent quantities. The weighted growth ratio $G$ compounds more than one generation of growth into a week, and the renewal-type ratio $R_A$ maps weekly growth back to the generation scale through an assumed generation interval (Section~\ref{sec:meth-stats}). Their intercepts therefore need a calibration before they can be compared with $v_R$. We built this calibration in a simulation that reproduces the observed panel (Section~\ref{sec:meth-inversion}). It keeps the four seasons, the rule-window lengths and the initial admissions of each state, and adds a national week shock that carries 45\% of $v_R$ and the delay from infection to admission. We simulated 200 panels at each of four values of $v_R$. The mean fitted intercept rose linearly with $v_R$ for both statistics (Fig.~\ref{fig:inversion}A), and no cell mean departed from the fitted line by more than 0.60 Monte Carlo standard errors.

Expressed as a scale factor on $v_R/\Rbar^2$, the fitted intercept was $\kappa=0.516$ for $R_A$ and 2.729 for $G$, close to the noise-free limits of 0.493 and 2.745 (Fig.~\ref{fig:inversion}B). A simpler design with 500 independent states and six observation weeks gave noise-free limits of 0.485 and 2.698, in line with the medians of 0.484 and 2.68 over the 17 scenarios of the first simulation. The data-matched panels also reproduced the dependence structure of the data. The intraclass correlation of weekly losses within calendar weeks was 0.258 for $R_A$ and 0.310 for $G$ in simulation, against 0.253 and 0.310 in the data, and the national-week share of the simulated $R_A$ intercept was 0.43, against 0.41 observed. Across settings that changed one ingredient at a time, $\kappa$ for $R_A$ moved most with the true generation interval (1.287 for a mean of 2.2 days and 0.380 for 4.3 days, with the statistic still computed at 3.6 days), with autocorrelation of transmission between weeks (0.780 at $\phi=0.5$) and with a synchronous shock to reported counts (1.041). It changed little with the national share of the shock, the admission delay or the offspring dispersion (0.454 to 0.535; Table~\ref{tab:s8}).

Mapping each empirical intercept through its calibration line gave an implied latent variance of $v_R^{\mathrm{impl}}=0.040$ (95\% CI 0.026 to 0.056) for $R_A$ and 0.043 (95\% CI 0.025 to 0.064) for $G$ in the baseline setting, in which the true mean generation interval equals the 3.6 days assumed by the statistic (Fig.~\ref{fig:inversion}A, C). Bootstrap variation in the empirical intercept accounted for nearly all of this width, since the interval from calibration error alone was 0.0397 to 0.0406 for $R_A$. The scale factors of the two statistics differ by a factor of 5.3. Their implied variances differed by $-0.003$ (95\% CI $-0.012$ to 0.005), a ratio of 0.93 (95\% CI 0.78 to 1.18). The 90\% interval of this ratio, 0.81 to 1.13, lies within 0.80 to 1.25, so two one-sided tests at the 5\% level declare the two estimates equivalent within a margin of 20\%. This agreement is a weak check on the calibration, because the two statistics also agreed in nine of the ten simulation settings, whose implied variances differed by a factor of up to 4.8 (Table~\ref{tab:s8}). On the latent scale, Proposition~\ref{prop:average} at the simulator values ($\Rbar=1.18$, $\rho=\rho_h=0.01$, $k=0.35$) gives a balance scale of 30.3 admissions per generation (95\% CI 21.7 to 46.3) for $R_A$, or 58.9 (95\% CI 42.3 to 90.0) per week at 1.94 generations per week. The apparent ratio of 37.4 is defined on the scale of $R_A$ and does not estimate this latent quantity directly.

The implied variance depended most on the true generation interval, which the interval of the baseline estimate does not include (Fig.~\ref{fig:envelope}B; Table~\ref{tab:s8}). For $R_A$ it was 0.013 (95\% CI 0.008 to 0.019) with a true mean of 2.2 days, 0.026 (95\% CI 0.017 to 0.036) with 2.9 days and 0.058 (95\% CI 0.038 to 0.081) with 4.3 days. With the baseline interval and first-order autocorrelation of 0.25 or 0.5 between weeks it was 0.033 (95\% CI 0.021 to 0.047) and 0.027 (95\% CI 0.017 to 0.040). Values for $G$ were close to those for $R_A$ in each setting, 0.014 and 0.027 at 2.2 and 2.9 days. Each setting changed one element of the simulator, so this envelope does not cover joint changes. The latent weekly balance scale moves inversely with the implied variance, from 35.3 admissions per week at 4.3 days to 110.8 at 2.9 days and 273.4 at 2.2 days. The 95\% interval of the difference between the two statistics contained zero in nine of the ten settings of Table~\ref{tab:s8}; the exception replaced the transmission shock by a synchronous multiplicative shock to reported counts, which gave 0.019 for $R_A$ and 0.029 for $G$ (ratio 0.67; 95\% CI 0.39 to 0.90).

Three further analyses examined the assumptions of the inversion (Table~\ref{tab:s10}). The lag-1 autocorrelation of deviations from state-season means was $-0.095$ (95\% CI $-0.168$ to $-0.019$) for $R_A$ and 0.123 (95\% CI 0.039 to 0.201) for $G$. Both statistics are autocorrelated by construction even when weekly shocks are independent; simulated panels with independent shocks gave means of $-0.074$ and 0.114, which placed the data at the 31st and 62nd percentiles. With a first-order coefficient of 0.25 the data fell at the 3rd and 10th percentiles, and with 0.5 below all simulated panels. The mean household serial interval of seasonal influenza has been estimated at 3.6 days (95\% CI 2.9 to 4.3) \citep{cowling2009estimation}, and direct estimates of the mean generation interval lie near the lower end of this interval \citep{chan2025estimating}. The autocorrelation of the data sat at the 2.5th and 9th percentiles of panels simulated with 2.9 days and at the 80th and 92nd percentiles with 4.3 days. This state-level check therefore does not favour the shorter interval, although it leaves the series of national-week means untested (Section~\ref{sec:meth-inversion}). The shorter interval did reproduce the empirical $b/a$. At $v_R=0.02$, the simulated value nearest its implied variance, the data sat at the 52.5th and 58th percentiles of the simulated $b/a$. Overdispersed weekly admissions with a variance-to-mean ratio of 1.5 or 2.0 kept the implied variance between 0.039 and 0.045. They raised the simulated $b/a$ at $v_R=0.04$ from 28.0 to 68.0 and 81.0 for $R_A$, and from 16.2 to 40.2 and 48.4 for $G$. These settings also gave lag-1 autocorrelations below the data for both statistics, so the data favour overdispersion smaller than a variance-to-mean ratio of 1.5.

On the scale of each statistic, the calibrated theory gave $b/a$ of 13.9 to 31.1 for $R_A$ and 8.0 to 18.8 for $G$ over eight combinations of $v_R\in\{0.04,0.08\}$, $k\in\{0.2,0.5\}$ and admission probability $\rho_h\in\{0.0056,0.021\}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:inversion}D). The empirical point estimates of 37.4 and 23.5 lie above these ranges, and their 95\% intervals overlap them. Ratios from single simulated panels varied widely; 95\% of them lay between 8.8 and 42.7 for $R_A$ and between 3.6 and 28.6 for $G$, and the empirical values fall at the 93rd and 91st percentiles of these distributions. Overdispersed admissions and a shorter generation interval can each raise the simulated ratio to the empirical values (Table~\ref{tab:s10}). The simulator gives every state the same intercept, and the within-state ratio of 17.3 lies inside the calibrated range for $R_A$ (Section~\ref{sec:res-fe}); spatial averaging in larger states is therefore a third candidate explanation of the high cross-sectional ratio.

\subsection{Multiscale decomposition of surveillance uncertainty}
\label{sec:res-multiscale}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_04_shared_variance_envelope.pdf}
  \caption{Shared variance and the dependence of the implied environmental variance on the generation interval. (A) Share of the intercept $a$ removed by national-week effects for $R_A$ and $G$, with 95\% two-way bootstrap intervals. (B) Implied environmental variance $v_R$ against the true mean generation interval of the calibration simulation, with both statistics computed at a mean of 3.6 days. Filled points assume independent weekly $R_t$ and carry 95\% intervals; open symbols give first-order autocorrelation of 0.25 and 0.5 at 3.6 days, offset horizontally for legibility. (C) Share of the 1748 state-weeks of $R_A$ with $C_t$ at or above the latent weekly balance scale implied at each generation interval; intervals are the shares at the ends of the 95\% interval of the scale. The dashed line is the share above the apparent ratio $b/a=37.4$.}
  \label{fig:envelope}
\end{figure}

The intercept contains a component shared by all states in a calendar week and a component specific to states (Fig.~\ref{fig:envelope}A; Fig.~\ref{fig:floor}C; Table~\ref{tab:s2}). Removing national-week effects reduced $a$ by 41\% (95\% CI 12 to 59) for $R_A$ and by 51\% (95\% CI 21 to 67) for $G$ in rule windows, and by 52\% for $G$ in calendar windows. These are shares of the intercept, the part of the loss that does not fall with case volume, and not of the total variance of the statistic. Removing state-season effects as well changed the intercept little; for $R_A$ it was 0.0083 (95\% CI 0.0064 to 0.0141) after both adjustments, and the interval for the state-season component included zero in every specification. The intraclass correlation of the loss within calendar weeks, which refers to the total loss, was 0.253 for $R_A$ and 0.310 for $G$. The bootstrap reflected the same structure. Resampling weeks alone gave a standard deviation of $\hat{a}$ of 0.0021 for $R_A$ and 0.0158 for $G$, 1.46 and 1.87 times the values from resampling states alone, and two-way resampling gave 0.0028 and 0.0193.

The national-week component enters the loss of every state in the same week. More admissions in one state reduce only that state's count term, and averaging over states reduces only the state-specific term, so neither removes the shared component; only further weeks average it out. Pooling the mean weekly deviation over $J$ randomly chosen states reduced its variance to 0.39, 0.31 and 0.25 of the single-state value for $R_A$ at $J=5$, 10 and 51, and to 0.43, 0.36 and 0.31 for $G$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}C). These values approach the national-week component, which is 0.23 of single-state variance for $R_A$ and 0.29 for $G$, and come within 10\% of it at $J=33$ and $J=25$ respectively, as Eq.~(\ref{eq:pooling}) predicts for partly synchronised variation.

\subsection{Mandatory and voluntary reporting regimes}
\label{sec:res-reporting}

Among state-weeks with a reporting scale of at least five, the median share of hospitals reporting was 92.3\% in the first mandatory period, 45.7\% under voluntary reporting and 92.9\% after mandatory reporting resumed (Fig.~\ref{fig:reporting}A). In the rule windows the median was 93.7\%, and 79.8\% of state-weeks had at least 90\% of hospitals reporting. Within these windows, adjusting each statistic for the reporting share changed the intercept of $R_A$ by $\Delta a=0.29\times10^{-3}$ (90\% CI $-0.22\times10^{-3}$ to $0.85\times10^{-3}$) and that of $G$ by $0.37\times10^{-3}$ (90\% CI $-2.83\times10^{-3}$ to $2.81\times10^{-3}$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:reporting}B; Table~\ref{tab:s3}). Both changes were equivalent to zero within margins set at a relative reporting volatility of $\tau_0=0.10$. These margins equal 17\% (95\% CI 12 to 25) of the intercept of $R_A$ and 12\% (95\% CI 8 to 20) of that of $G$, and they depend on the sample mean of each statistic through Eq.~(\ref{eq:tost}). The measured change was 2.0\% of the intercept for $R_A$ (95\% CI $-2.4$ to 6.1; one-sided 95\% upper bound 5.3\%, the 95th percentile of the bootstrap distribution of the share) and 0.4\% for $G$ (95\% CI $-4.4$ to 4.0; upper bound 3.5\%), and all four seasons for $R_A$ and three of four for $G$ were equivalent individually. At $\tau_0=0.05$, with margins of about 4\% and 3\% of the intercepts, neither change was equivalent, and the power of the test at a true change of zero was 0.25 for $R_A$ and 0 for $G$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:reporting}C). Because most state-weeks in the windows had nearly complete participation, these tests bound the effect of small departures from full participation and do not extend to low participation.

The voluntary period gave the only contrast in which reporting completeness was low. Over May to October, the interquartile range of $\log G$ was 0.759 in 2024 under voluntary reporting, against 0.479 in 2023 and 0.483 in 2025 (Fig.~\ref{fig:reporting}D; Table~\ref{tab:s4}). The ratio of 2024 to 2023 was 1.58 (95\% CI 0.95 to 2.23) and that of 2024 to 2025 was 1.57 (95\% CI 0.95 to 2.39), whereas the placebo ratio of 2025 to 2023 was 1.01 (95\% CI 0.72 to 1.25). Median reporting scales in these months were similar (15.6, 16.6 and 16.2 admissions), and matching state-weeks on reporting scale gave ratios of 1.68 and 1.58. Matching on calendar month gave a median ratio of 1.33, with the ratio above one in nine of twelve month-by-year comparisons. COVID-19 admissions showed ratios of 1.62 against 2023 and 1.18 against 2025, and RSV admissions a ratio of 1.36 against 2025. All of these comparisons use the same off-season of 2024 and are not independent replications. In 44 states with laboratory data, laboratory $R_A$ correlated with hospital $R_A$ at $r=0.31$ and shared 0.230 (95\% CI 0.043 to 0.418) of the hospital intercept.

\subsection{Diminishing returns and surveillance design}
\label{sec:res-design}

Under $V(m)=a+b/m$, the share of the precision attainable at unlimited case volume is $m/(m+\mx)$, and one doubling of $m$ reduces variance by the fraction $\mx/[2(m+\mx)]$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:average}). The share is 33\%, 50\%, 67\% and 80\% at $m=0.5$, 1, 2 and 4 times $\mx$, a doubling from these points cuts variance by 33\%, 25\%, 17\% and 10\%, and 90\% of attainable precision requires $m=9\mx$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}B). With the apparent balance ratios of 37.4 for $R_A$ and 23.5 for $G$, 55\% and 65\% of state-weeks in the rule windows lay above the ratio of the corresponding statistic. These shares refer to the apparent scale. On the latent scale, 44\% (95\% CI 35 to 52) of state-weeks of $R_A$ lay at or above the weekly balance scale of 58.9 admissions implied in the baseline setting. The share was 31\% with a true mean generation interval of 2.9 days, 14\% with 2.2 days and 57\% with 4.3 days (Fig.~\ref{fig:envelope}C). For a single state, the number of observation weeks needed to reach a given relative standard error of mean $R_A$ fell steeply with admissions below $\mx$ and changed little above about $4\mx$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}A). At the apparent balance ratio and a cost ratio of $c_0/c_1=10$, the optimal case volume was $m^*=19.4$ (95\% CI 13.8 to 26.6) for $R_A$ and 15.3 for $G$; at $c_0/c_1=100$ it was 61.2 and 48.4.

The fitted variance departs from that of a Poisson renewal model, in which the error variance of the estimate is $\Rbar/m$ and has no term that persists as $m$ grows (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}D). On the apparent scale of $R_A$, the fitted slope was 1.25 times the Poisson value of 0.446, and the fitted variance was twice the Poisson variance at $m=22$ and ten times at $m=261$. On the latent scale of Proposition~\ref{prop:average}, with $v_R=0.040$, the corresponding values were $m=28$ and $m=262$.

Seasons limit any estimate of variation between seasons (Fig.~\ref{fig:seasons}). Season-specific intercepts of $R_A$ were 0.0137, 0.0062, 0.0127 and 0.0225, with a random-effects mean of 0.0125 (95\% CI 0.0022 to 0.0227) and $I^2=0.47$ (Table~\ref{tab:s5}). For $G$ the random-effects mean was 0.0619 (95\% CI 0.0066 to 0.1171) in rule windows and 0.0735 (95\% CI $-0.0558$ to 0.2028) in calendar windows. The relative standard error of any unbiased estimate of the between-season variance is at least 1.00 with three seasons and 0.82 with four (Corollary~\ref{cor:season}). Intercept ratios for alternative denominator weights showed $q_1<1<q_2$ for influenza, COVID-19 and RSV, for example $q_1=0.688$ and $q_2=1.539$ for influenza in calendar windows (Table~\ref{tab:s6}). Epidemic growth with reporting noise and no environmental variation gave ranges of 0.63 to 0.89 for $q_1$ and 1.13 to 1.58 for $q_2$ in calendar windows, so this ordering does not discriminate between mechanisms. Of 40 one-sided tests, 21 were significant before correction, 18 after Benjamini--Hochberg correction and 10 after Holm correction, all of them for influenza and none for departures from the null ranges.
