\begin{abstract}
\noindent This study asks why additional reported cases sharpen an estimate of the current reproduction number but do much less for an estimate of transmission across periods. In a branching process with environmental variation, overdispersed offspring and binomial reporting, a conditionally unbiased estimate from $m$ reported cases has mean squared error $A/m$ about the current value $R_t$ and $v_R+A/m$ about the mean across periods, because true variation of $R_t$ between periods is irreducible sampling variation for the second target. Precision for that target levels off beyond the balance scale $\mx=A/v_R$. Volatile reporting adds a term independent of $m$. With independent periods and linear costs, a fixed budget is better spent on further periods once $m$ exceeds $\sqrt{(c_0/c_1)\mx}$. In US influenza admissions during early growth in four seasons, an affine loss model gave an apparent balance ratio of 37.4 admissions per week (95\% CI 19.1 to 71.0), and a power law fitted about as well. Binomial thinning steepened the slope at 0.41 (95\% CI 0.31 to 0.53), compatible with the Poisson value of 0.45. State intercepts gave 17.3 (95\% CI 3.3 to 51.4), not distinguishable from the cross-sectional value. Simulation-based calibration gave an environmental variance of 0.040 (95\% CI 0.026 to 0.056) for a baseline mean generation interval of 3.6 days and 0.013 to 0.026 for 2.2 to 2.9 days. Between 41\% and 51\% of the intercept was shared by all states within a week, and 55\% of state-weeks lay above the apparent ratio and about 44\% above the latent scale.

\end{abstract}
