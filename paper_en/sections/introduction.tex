\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The time-varying reproduction number $R_t$ is a cornerstone metric for tracking epidemic dynamics and guiding public health interventions \citep{wallinga2004different, fraser2007estimating, cori2013new}. An estimate exceeding unity indicates an expanding outbreak, whereas values below unity signal contraction. While extensive methodological research has refined the statistical estimation of $R_t$ from given incidence time series---addressing sliding window choices, reporting delays, generation interval distributions, and truncation adjustments \citep{thompson2019improved, gostic2020practical, nouvellet2018simple, steyn2025primer}---surveillance system designers face an antecedent question: how many infections must be captured, across how many facilities and periods, to attain a designated level of inferential precision?

This question is acute in severe-outcome surveillance, such as hospitalisation monitoring. Severe cases represent a small and variable fraction of total infections---typically 0.6\%--2\% for seasonal influenza \citep{zhou2012hospitalizations, tokars2018seasonal}. In systems such as the US National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN), weekly hospital admissions in smaller jurisdictions frequently number only dozens of cases, and reporting protocols may alternate between mandatory and voluntary regimes \citep{cdc2026nhsn}. As multi-model collaborative ensembles and scoring rules are deployed for real-time forecasting \citep{reich2019accuracy, cramer2022evaluation, mathis2024evaluation, bosse2023scoring}, surveillance authorities require quantitative guidance on resource allocation: what fraction of estimation error can be eliminated by expanding patient volume, whether raising reporting completeness is preferable to broadening population coverage, and at what scale returns on sampling begin to diminish.

Classical renewal theory offers an deceptively straightforward answer. Under a Poisson renewal process, the Fisher information for the reproduction number grows linearly with observed case counts, implying that estimation variance scales inversely with sample volume \citep{parag2022quantifying}. Superspreading and overdispersion in secondary infections, typically parameterised through a negative-binomial offspring distribution \citep{lloydsmith2005superspreading, endo2020estimating, kucharski2020early}, increase the variance prefactor but preserve its $1/m$ scaling rate. Under this framework, expanding case volume yields smooth, monotonic precision gains, with no structural scale beyond which single-period sampling becomes unremunerative.

In real-world surveillance, two distinct noise mechanisms invalidate this intuition. The first is environmental stochasticity. Hosts in the same jurisdiction and time interval share exogenous transmission conditions, including meteorology, school calendars, and population mobility \citep{dalziel2018urbanization, rasmussen2011inference}. Ecology distinguishes environmental stochasticity, which affects all individuals simultaneously, from demographic stochasticity, which operates independently across individuals \citep{engen1998demographic, rohani2002determinism}. While the relative contribution of demographic stochasticity vanishes as sample size increases, environmental stochasticity does not attenuate with population volume. Increasing case counts refines our estimate of the instantaneous transmission potential $R_t$ in a specific environment, but cannot eliminate the cross-period variation of $R_t$ itself. When surveillance aims to infer the underlying typical transmission potential across periods or to forecast the subsequent generation, environmental variance forms an irreducible error component under single-period scale expansion.

This issue is conceptually distinct from the difference between true and apparent reproduction numbers elucidated by \citet{eales2024differences}. \citet{eales2024differences} demonstrated that an apparent reproduction number reconstructed from downstream surveillance proxies (such as hospitalisations or deaths) is delayed and smoothed relative to the true transmission potential due to convolution with delay kernels, even when case counts are infinite and observation is deterministic. In our framework, the two inferential targets are the conditional instantaneous value and the cross-period mean; while both are affected by convolution when using lagged surveillance counts to construct empirical growth statistics (Section~\ref{sec:meth-renewal}), environmental variance is an intrinsic attribute of biological transmission rather than an artefact of deconvolution.

The second noise mechanism arises from reporting heterogeneity. The fraction of cases recorded by surveillance fluctuates across periods due to changing healthcare-seeking behaviour, diagnostic testing availability, and institutional compliance. Such fluctuations act multiplicatively across all cases in a given period and cannot be averaged out by sampling more patients within that interval. Prior contributions have quantified the information content of epidemic curves and developed state-space models and filtering techniques for time-varying transmission \citep{azmon2014estimating, stoner2019hierarchical, bracher2021marginal, bhatt2023semi, flaxman2020estimating, parag2021improved, parag2022fundamental, parag2025asymmetric}. However, existing formulations have not integrated environmental and reporting variances into an explicit design framework, leaving unanswered where sampling returns diminish, how reporting noise interacts with environmental fluctuations, and how surveillance budgets should be balanced across sample volume, observation periods, and data harmonisation.

Here, we formulate a negative-binomial transmission and binomial observation framework that formally separates two inferential targets: the conditional instantaneous reproduction number $R_t$ (Target 1) and the marginal cross-period mean $\bar{R}$ (Target 2). For Target 1, the natural estimator $\widehat{R}_t = C_t/m$ is conditionally unbiased and attains the Cram\'er--Rao lower bound, with variance decaying strictly as $1/m$. For Target 2, any conditionally unbiased estimator retains an environmental variance floor $v_R$, yielding a total variance $V(m) = v_R + A/m$. We derive the variance balance scale $m_\times = A/v_R$, which identifies the scale where sampling and environmental variances balance. We extend this decomposition to account for reporting heterogeneity $v_H$, demonstrate an inherent unidentifiability between environmental and reporting variances from count moments alone, and characterise an analytical reporting signature that permits mechanistic diagnosis. Finally, we establish optimal budget allocation rules across sample volume, temporal replication, and reporting standardisation, and validate these theoretical scaling laws using 10,303 state-weeks of US influenza hospitalisations from CDC NHSN alongside cross-pathogen datasets.
